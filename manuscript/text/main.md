# Does learning from other species improve species distribution models in new regions?

<div id="authors"></div>

<!-- Working manuscript, 18 October 2026. Accepted analyses are unchanged. Drafting and review are recorded in docs/plan/ManuscriptDrafting-20261018.md. -->

## Abstract

1. Multi-species pretraining could improve species distribution models by learning environmental relationships from other species. Yet a pretrained network can outperform a conventional model even when shared learning adds little. We combined target-excluded pretraining, matched target-only networks and withheld geographic assessments to test whether the contribution of pretraining persists in new regions.

2. Three experiments used cell-level environmental predictors: eight Tasmanian mammals, five mainland mammals transferred to Tasmania, and 157 south-eastern Australian birds. The primary bird panel comprised 73 species evaluated in three whole bioregions excluded from development with 50-km buffers. Primary models and scoring rules were fixed before held responses were examined. Each fine-tuned network was compared with a matched target-only network and tuned conventional models.

3. Pretraining improved development discrimination, measured by area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC), by less than 0.01 in mainland mammals and birds. Neither experiment retained a positive mean increment in the held geographies. In the held bird regions, fine-tuning exceeded conventional learning by 0.0028 AUC; target-only learning exceeded it by 0.0032. Most of the gain from averaging neural and conventional predictions was also available without pretraining. Post hoc regional scoring of unchanged development predictions produced a decrease in AUC similar to the development–transfer gap, showing why that gap alone cannot measure geographic failure. In a post hoc Tasmanian evaluation, restricting negatives to survey-supported nondetections reversed which of the fine-tuned and conventional models ranked higher by mean AUC. Practical rankings therefore depended on how negative locations were defined.

4. With these tabular predictors, a neural model's predictive advantage persisted in new regions without an average pretraining benefit. Claims of benefit from learning across species therefore need matched controls and assessment across the geographic and observation boundaries the application will cross.

**Keywords:** geographic transfer; multi-species learning; observation process; presence–background models; spatial validation; transfer learning.

## Introduction

Species distribution models are often asked to predict beyond the places where species have been recorded. Multi-species pretraining promises to help by learning environmental relationships from other species before adapting a network to a focal one. A successful prediction can nevertheless have several explanations. Shared learning may contribute, a network trained only on the target may already capture the useful relationships, or the evaluation may reward contrasts that are easier than those encountered in a new region. These possibilities lead to different decisions about whether the extra data and fitting required for pretraining are worthwhile.

Shared models have improved predictions in several ecological settings, although gains depend on the prediction task and biological level [@norberg2019]. Multi-species embeddings and neural joint species models learn shared structure [@harris2015; @chen2017], while deep distribution models use environmental and spatial context at regional to global scales [@botella2018; @deneu2021; @cole2023]. Recent work extends these approaches to plant communities [@brun2024], transfers presence-only representations to abundance prediction [@bourel2025], and borrows information from common species to predict rare ones [@ovaskainen2025]. Few-shot range estimation also shows that representations learned without the evaluation species can support predictions for previously unseen species [@lange2025]. Sequential deep learning has been evaluated with external plant records across geographic scales [@santos2026]. Among these close precedents, we found no test combining target-excluded auxiliary pretraining, a matched target-only network and whole-region geographic exclusion. That combination asks whether sharing information improves a target's predictions where the model has not been developed.

For a practitioner deciding whether to pretrain a model, two questions remain distinct. Does the pretrained network predict better than the same network fitted only to the focal species? And does the resulting model improve on a credible alternative such as a tuned tree ensemble? A neural model may outperform or complement a conventional model because of its architecture, even when pretraining adds little. Conversely, a positive pretraining increment may leave it behind a simpler competitor. Same-architecture or no-transfer comparisons have precedents [@chen2017; @bourel2025]; their value is to identify the contribution of sharing information rather than attribute every neural-model gain to it.

That contribution also needs to be tested across the intended prediction boundary. Spatial cross-validation can reduce dependence between training and assessment data, but its partitions must represent the intended application [@roberts2017]. Predicting withheld locations within a sampled domain and predicting a whole new region impose different demands [@wenger2012; @yates2018]. Even the apparent size of a transfer failure depends on how performance is measured. AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen recorded presence receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative or background location. Across a large domain, some of those pairs span very different environments; within a region, such comparisons disappear. Absolute AUC can therefore change with evaluation extent without any change in the predictions [@lobo2008; @jimenezvalverde2012].

Species records introduce a further boundary. Many datasets identify where a species was recorded but do not establish where it was absent. Models then distinguish records from background locations, and can learn environmental associations with recording as well as with habitat [@phillips2009; @warton2013]. Records of other species can establish that a cell received some sampling, but cannot make every unrecorded focal species a true absence. A shared representation could be particularly sensitive to this common observation process. Testing against well-recorded background and survey-supported nondetections helps reveal that dependence without identifying its cause.

Here we test the added predictive value of target-excluded multi-species pretraining across three ecological settings. We focus on cell-level climate and habitat summaries of the kind used by conventional SDMs, so that the comparison asks what pretraining adds with the same environmental information. This is a test of tabular environmental representations, not imagery or spatial foundation models. We combine matched target-only neural controls, tuned conventional comparators and geographic assessments kept outside model development. The most extensive experiment uses 157 bird targets, with a common 73-species panel evaluated in three whole withheld bioregions. Learning curves ask whether less target data or more auxiliary species strengthens the benefit. Diagnostics then examine whether evaluation extent, recording richness, model averaging or negative-class construction changes its interpretation. The contribution is a controlled empirical test of the geographic value of pretraining, with explicit separation of the benefit of shared learning from the performance of the final predictor.

## Methods

### Ecological settings and prediction boundaries

The three experiments were specified separately and are synthesised descriptively (Table 1). They differ in ecological scope, response construction and transfer task. Shared mammal taxa, observation sources and Tasmanian geography preclude treating them as independent replicates of a common effect. Throughout, *development* denotes evaluation within the retained data using spatial folds; *outer assessment* denotes the separately withheld geography.

Occurrence records for 2001–2025 were accessed through the Atlas of Living Australia [@belbin2021ala], with private camera-survey evidence contributing to the Tasmanian responses. Acquisition dates, taxonomic authorities, environmental products and unresolved source metadata are listed in Supplementary Methods S4. The mainland and bird experiments shared a versioned environmental substrate, including WorldClim 2.1 climate summaries [@fick2017worldclim]; the original Tasmania experiment used a separate legacy stack.

The original Tasmania experiment modelled eight mammal species on a 0.01-degree grid, combining recorded presences, camera survey-supported nondetections and constructed pseudoabsences. Unknown species–cell combinations were masked. Its 12,711 resolved response rows included 11,039 for development and 1,672 in five patches fixed before modelling. The patches contained 606 geographic cells, touched training areas and had no external buffer. They therefore assess interpolation within Tasmania. Camera nondetection supports a sampled negative response but does not prove ecological absence.

The mainland experiment pretrained on 19 auxiliary mammal response heads for each of five target species. Its mainland background required records of at least three other support species and separation of at least 5 km from a target presence. Tasmania supplied neither training responses nor fitted transformations. The primary transfer assessment used 4,077 camera-supported species–cell rows in Tasmania; an all-source assessment was secondary. This transfer changes observation source as well as geography.

The bird experiment used 505 recording-support donor taxa and 259 eligible auxiliary-head or target candidates. The accepted occurrence substrate contained 5,048,061 unique species–cell memberships. Support criteria admitted 157 targets, of which 73 were evaluable in every outer region. All three regions and their 50-km external buffers were excluded simultaneously from development. The regions were selected for different prediction settings before model outcomes were available (Table 1); those roles do not isolate the causal effect of an environmental property. The regional secondary panels contained 88, 122 and 134 targets in Naracoorte Coastal Plain, South Eastern Highlands and NSW North Coast, respectively.

Bird positives were recorded presences. Primary background cells had no focal record and at least ten other donor species recorded; requiring 20 donors supplied an evaluation sensitivity. Recording richness was not a predictor. The models estimate discrimination of recorded presence from supported background, rather than occupancy or true prevalence.

### Isolating the contribution of pretraining

All networks received cell-level environmental summaries. The original Tasmania schema had 28 raw predictors and 77 encoded columns. Birds used 24 variables: 19 bioclimatic variables, elevation mean and standard deviation, vegetation group, soil order and land-use class, yielding 103 encoded columns. The mainland experiment used its separately fixed portable schema. Scaling and categorical encoding were fitted on the training side of each partition. Bird development folds kept projected 50-km spatial blocks intact.

The deep species distribution model was a residual multilayer perceptron. A shared *encoder* transformed environmental inputs into features used by separate species prediction heads during pretraining. Auxiliary species contributed equal total weight while retaining their own class imbalance. The focal target was excluded from auxiliary learning. In birds, targets were grouped into ten computational blocks and the entire focal block was excluded from auxiliary heads and auxiliary recording-richness construction. Geographic exclusions applied to auxiliary learning as well as target fitting.

After pretraining, *fine-tuning* fitted a new target head and updated the shared encoder using the target's data. The central *target-only* control used the same architecture, target responses, optimisation and evaluation rules, starting from random weights. Their paired performance difference is the pretraining increment studied here. A frozen-encoder control fitted only a target head. Scalar controls supplied a target-excluded pooled community prediction as one additional covariate. These examine particular forms of information sharing; they do not represent every possible community model.

Five matched seeds were used for final neural comparisons, with predictions averaged before scoring. For birds, one shared architecture was selected from four fixed candidates using fine-tuning performance on the common 73-species development panel. The chosen network had six residual blocks of width 256 and a bottleneck of width 128. The target-only control inherited that architecture without a separate search. This asymmetry conditions the comparison on an architecture selected for fine-tuning and makes the development estimates vulnerable to selection optimism [@cawley2010]. Target-only nevertheless exceeded a separately tuned shallow neural network by mean development AUC 0.0037, while trailing the selected conventional model by 0.0024.

Conventional candidates comprised an intercept model, elastic-net logistic regression, random forest, histogram gradient boosting and a shallow neural network. Hyperparameters and the preferred family were selected within development data and fixed for outer prediction. Early stopping, replay and retained-data refitting likewise used development information only. No outer response selected a model, epoch, ensemble weight or threshold. Model specifications, grids and software versions are given in Supplementary Methods and Table S3.

Target-data curves used 25%, 50% and 100% of available training data. Tasmania used three nested subset ladders at reduced fractions, producing seven endpoints. Birds crossed the common panel's target fractions with 20, 80 or the full eligible auxiliary-species pool. Increasing that pool changes record volume and species composition together; it is not an isolated manipulation of species richness. Subsampling well-recorded targets also differs from modelling naturally rare species.

### Performance and diagnostic analyses

ROC AUC was primary for mainland transfer and birds. AUC and true skill statistic (TSS; sensitivity plus specificity minus one) were co-primary in Tasmania. TSS used thresholds chosen by a fixed maximum-TSS rule from development out-of-fold predictions, then held fixed for outer evaluation. Supplementary metrics included Matthews correlation and source-specific specificity. AUC assesses score ranking; TSS assesses performance at one operating threshold, so their conclusions need not coincide.

Primary bird summaries average the three regional AUCs within each species, then weight species equally. Development AUC averages folds within species. We report paired species contrasts, means, medians and positive-species counts, with all species retained. These are descriptive because species share geography and auxiliary models. Post hoc single-species deletion and paired species-composition resampling describe sensitivity to the observed panel's composition (Table S1). The resampling ranges in Fig. 1 are not confidence intervals and omit uncertainty from fitting, spatial sampling and selection. A near-zero mean does not establish equivalence.

An exploratory development analysis compared bird discrimination against background containing 10–24, 25–99 or at least 100 other recorded donor species. All focal presences were reused across the three bins. A corresponding outer diagnostic, including common-species support rules, was fixed before opening outer responses. A second outer diagnostic restricted both classes to cells with at least 100 other recorded donors. These are tests of dependence on recording support, not estimates of sampling effort or occupancy.

Two further bird analyses were added after outer opening. First, we recomputed AUC from unchanged development predictions across the whole retained domain, within named retained bioregions, and within region-by-fold strata. Regional and region-by-fold scoring required at least 30 and 20 cells per class, respectively, and averaged eligible strata equally within species. Regional scoring excludes the unnamed domain complement and changes comparison pairs and weights. Second, we compared a target-only/conventional equal-weight ensemble with the fine-tuned/conventional equal-weight ensemble specified before opening as exploratory. Both use saved scores without fitted ensemble weights.

The original Tasmania protocol proposed removing pseudoabsences from target training. Its implementation across all eight species and seven endpoints was specified after primary outer opening. It removed 368 full-data training rows while retaining the original evaluation panel, architecture, auxiliary states and selected conventional candidates. Target models were refitted and thresholds reselected from their development predictions. A subsequent post hoc evaluation retained all 1,300 held presences and 242 survey-supported negatives, excluding 130 held pseudoabsences. It reused the closed predictions and thresholds from both training regimes. This separates changes in training responses from changes in evaluation composition; it does not validate the survey negatives as true absences. Details, source counts and map comparisons are in Tables S5–S10.

## Results

### Small development gains did not establish an average geographic advantage

Pretraining modestly improved development predictions for mainland mammals and birds (Fig. 1). For the five mainland targets, mean AUC was 0.9091 after fine-tuning, 0.9004 for target-only learning and 0.9052 for conventional learning. The paired gains were +0.0087 over target-only, positive for four species, and +0.0040 over conventional learning, positive for all five. For the common 73 birds, the corresponding AUCs were 0.8316, 0.8258 and 0.8282. Fine-tuning improved on target-only by +0.0058 in the mean and for 72 species; its mean conventional-model advantage was +0.0034, positive for 67 species.

Neither experiment retained a positive mean pretraining increment in the held geographies. Mainland-to-Tasmania AUC averaged 0.5560 for fine-tuning, 0.5715 for target-only and 0.5837 for conventional learning. Fine-tuning minus target-only was −0.0155 in the mean, although three of five species benefited and the median was +0.0269. Leave-one-species-out means ranged from −0.0435 to +0.0169. This small panel therefore gives a heterogeneous transfer result, not a general negative effect across mammals.

Across the three held bird regions, mean AUC was 0.6827 for fine-tuning and 0.6831 for target-only: a paired difference of −0.0004, positive for 34 of 73 species. The regional increments were +0.0064 in NCP, −0.0040 in SEH and −0.0035 in NNC. The sole positive regional mean occurred in the region assigned the environmentally supported transfer role. Against conventional learning, fine-tuning averaged +0.0028 overall, but its regional contrast also changed sign (+0.0078, −0.0071 and +0.0076). Even within SEH, the negative mean pretraining increment coexisted with a positive median of +0.0021 and 41 positive species. Regional averages conceal consequential negative tails. The target-only network also exceeded conventional learning in the held regions, by +0.0032 AUC on average. The advantage over conventional learning was therefore available without pretraining; the comparison with conventional learning alone would not establish a benefit from borrowing across species.

The development increments remained positive under the observed-panel resampling exercises, whereas both outer contrast ranges crossed zero (Fig. 1; Table S1). Fixed-threshold bird TSS also differed from the AUC comparison: 0.2018 for fine-tuning versus 0.1920 for target-only.

The original Tasmania experiment had no consistent development AUC advantage from pretraining: full-data mean contrasts were −0.0068 against target-only and −0.0498 against conventional learning. In the held patches, fine-tuning also trailed target-only by mean AUC (−0.0147) while exceeding it by co-primary TSS (+0.0477). Only one of eight species had a positive AUC contrast, compared with five positive TSS contrasts and one tie. Ranking and fixed-threshold performance thus gave different assessments of benefit.

Within the bird development data, reducing target records increased fine-tuning's advantage over conventional learning: +0.0069 at 25%, +0.0053 at 50% and +0.0034 at full data. For scale, the conventional pipeline gained +0.0037 mean AUC when its target data doubled from 50% to 100% (0.8245 to 0.8282), although model selection differed by fraction and neural/conventional seeds were unpaired. A post hoc comparison found that half-data fine-tuning exceeded full-data conventional learning by +0.0016 on average. However, the primary panel's median 14,063 retained presences meant that the quarter-data fits still used roughly 2,800 presences. In the least-recorded third of all 157 targets—53 species with 224–3,649 presence cells—the full-data advantage was only +0.0006 over conventional learning, despite a +0.0041 increment over target-only. Evidence for data efficiency in the main panel therefore did not establish a comparable practical benefit for the least-recorded species.

Auxiliary learning did supply reusable development information: the frozen-encoder contrast with target-only rose from −0.0044 with 20 auxiliary species to +0.0022 with the full pool, with all 73 targets improving. Full fine-tuning instead peaked at 80 auxiliary species; the pool changes species composition and record volume together.

### Evaluation extent and model averaging change the interpretation of performance

The absolute bird AUC decline was strongly dependent on the comparison extent (Fig. 2a). Using unchanged development predictions, fine-tuned AUC was 0.8327 across the whole retained domain, 0.6911 when averaged within named regions and 0.6897 within region-by-fold strata. The last value was close to the held-region mean of 0.6827. Regional scoring excluded 360,625 domain-complement cells and changed which presence–background pairs contributed to AUC.

The pretraining increment nevertheless remained positive under both regional aggregations: +0.0110 within regions and +0.0072 within region-by-fold strata, positive for 56 and 68 of 73 species, respectively. Thus broad-domain ranking pairs alone did not account for the development advantage that was absent, on average, in the held regions.

Model averaging gave a clearer practical gain (Fig. 2b). The fine-tuned/conventional ensemble reached outer AUC 0.6903, an increase of +0.0104 over conventional learning, positive for 60 of 73 species. The post hoc target-only/conventional ensemble reached 0.6896, an increase of +0.0096, positive for 64 species. The two ensembles differed by only +0.0007 in the mean, with the pretrained ensemble favoured for 37 species. Most of the ensemble improvement was therefore available without multi-species pretraining. This does not establish identical information in the two networks; it identifies an alternative route to almost the same average gain.

### The definition of negative observations changed practical rankings

The development richness diagnostic retained 59 primary bird species with support in every background bin. Their mean fine-tuning advantage over conventional learning declined from +0.0050 against cells containing 10–24 other donors to +0.0019 for 25–99 and −0.0039 for at least 100 (Fig. 3; Table S2). The matched target-only contrasts followed the same progression: +0.0075, +0.0039 and −0.0043.

The prospectively fixed outer diagnostic showed a similar decline where all three bins were supported. For the common 38-species SEH panel, conventional-model contrasts were −0.0038, −0.0140 and −0.0283; for the common 55-species NNC panel, they were +0.0148, +0.0060 and −0.0259. NCP had no species supported in all three bins and supplies no corresponding comparison. Restricting both classes to high-richness cells also gave negative mean conventional-model contrasts in SEH and NNC (−0.0373 and −0.0079).

High-richness background was uncommon: the mean within-species shares were 0.28%, 0.61% and 2.77% in NCP, SEH and NNC, respectively. These diagnostics therefore concern a restricted part of the evaluation data. They show that model comparisons depend on recording support, while leaving environmental composition, biological richness and observation effort as competing explanations.

The Tasmania experiment provides a complementary comparison of constructed and survey-supported negatives. Removing constructed pseudoabsences from target training initially appeared detrimental: mean AUC on the unchanged mixed evaluation panel fell for all three methods (Table 2a). The interpretation reversed when evaluation was restricted to survey-supported negatives. On this post hoc panel, pseudoabsence removal raised mean AUC from 0.8352 to 0.8572 for fine-tuning, from 0.8403 to 0.8587 for target-only and from 0.8198 to 0.8385 for conventional learning. The training change's apparent benefit therefore depended on which negative observations defined the test.

The comparator mattered as well. Fine-tuning remained below target-only in mean AUC under both training regimes and both evaluation panels. Against conventional learning, however, its mean AUC contrast was negative on the mixed panel (−0.0051 under original training; −0.0071 after removal) and positive on the survey-only panel (+0.0154 and +0.0187). TSS retained a further distinction: pseudoabsence removal changed the target-only contrast from +0.0477 to −0.0182 on the mixed panel, but from +0.0550 to +0.0097 on the survey-only panel (Table 2b).

The largest mixed-panel AUC losses were concentrated in *Vombatus ursinus* and *Dasyurus maculatus*, whose negative classes contained 18/25 and 29/50 constructed pseudoabsences. Removing training pseudoabsences also changed mapped rankings: among the seven affected species, models retained 31.4–85.5% of the original highest-scoring decile of cells. ## Discussion

Learning from other species improved development predictions for mainland mammals and birds, but on average that increment did not carry into the held geography. The bird assessment shows why the matched control matters. In the held regions, the fine-tuned network edged out tuned conventional models by +0.0028 AUC, yet the same network trained without pretraining had an advantage of +0.0032. The neural model's small edge in new places was available without borrowing information from other species. A conventional-model comparison alone would therefore have supported the predictor while leaving the contribution of pretraining unresolved.

The result qualifies a particular use of shared learning. Multi-species embeddings, common-to-rare information transfer and pretrained representations already have demonstrated predictive value [@chen2017; @brun2024; @bourel2025; @ovaskainen2025; @lange2025]. Our test asks whether target-excluded environmental pretraining adds value when an otherwise matched model must predict across a geographic boundary. Together, species exclusion, matched controls and withheld regions separate the value of the shared representation from the performance of the final predictor. The positive bird result in NCP and heterogeneous mainland species contrasts also rule out a blanket assertion that pretraining cannot transfer. The supported conclusion is narrower: the small development advantage did not persist on average under the geographic assessments tested here.

The ensemble comparison makes the practical distinction particularly clear. Fine-tuning plus conventional learning produced better average held-region discrimination than either constituent, but nearly the same improvement was available from a target-only network plus conventional learning. For an application seeking accurate predictions, that second combination deserves consideration without assuming an additional benefit from auxiliary pretraining. For a study claiming a benefit from multi-species learning, ensemble improvement alone is insufficient evidence. This is consistent with broader evidence that SDM rankings depend on the task and on how individual learners are fitted [@norberg2019; @valavi2022].

Evaluation extent explains another potential misreading. The large absolute difference between broad-domain and held-region AUC could appear to show a severe failure of transfer. Yet much of that difference was reproduced by evaluating unchanged development predictions within regions. The prediction task became harder because distant comparison pairs were removed. At the same time, the positive within-region development increment shows that this change does not explain away the pretraining result. These observations support comparing methods on the same evaluation rows and distinguishing absolute discrimination from the added value of pretraining. They do not partition the observed difference into a causal geographic penalty: development and outer assessments also differ in fitted models, environmental composition and recording support.

Changing the negative class altered model rankings as well as absolute discrimination. In the bird study, the advantage over conventional learning declined as background recording richness increased. In Tasmania, removing pseudoabsences—3.3% of training rows, or 16.4% of training negatives—reduced performance against the original mixture but improved performance against survey-supported negatives alone. The latter evaluation even reversed the mean AUC ranking of fine-tuning and conventional learning. Thus “better prediction” can depend on whether the task is to distinguish recorded presences from constructed background or from sampled nondetections. Neither is automatically an estimate of ecological absence. Reporting the negative class is therefore part of stating the prediction target, rather than a minor preprocessing detail.

The diagnostics do not reveal why these changes occurred. Recording richness combines survey activity, community richness and environmental composition. Removing pseudoabsences changes class balance, spatial support and target fitting; reselected thresholds add a calibration change for TSS. Shared representations may encode environmental correlates of recording, or fine-tuning may amplify associations particular to the retained domain. Feature distortion during fine-tuning is possible in other machine-learning settings [@kumar2022], but remains a hypothesis here. The survey-only comparison is source-conditional and post hoc, with sparse negative support for some species. It cannot identify a learned sampling mechanism or validate either set of maps.

Several design choices further bound the inference. We supplied tabular, cell-level environmental summaries, not imagery or a spatial foundation model. Spatial context can improve deep SDMs [@deneu2021], so these results should not be extended to every input representation. The bird architecture was selected using fine-tuning on the development panel, without independent architecture optimisation for target-only learning. A larger auxiliary pool simultaneously supplied more records and different species, preventing a pure species-number interpretation. Shared species, auxiliary pools and geography make the reported contrasts dependent. Finally, simulated record scarcity in well-recorded birds did not reproduce the limited conventional-model gain in the least-recorded panel. A general claim about rare-species modelling would require a design centred on those species.

The next useful tests would change the information available to the model or the evidence used to assess it. Observation-aware models and independent survey data could better distinguish habitat relationships from recording processes [@warton2013; @johnston2021]. Spatial habitat inputs could test whether the current limits arise partly from the cell-level representation. Both require a newly fixed selection and geographic evaluation design, because the present holdouts have already informed those hypotheses. Here, the neural model's advantage in held bird regions was available without pretraining, despite the consistent development increment from learning across species. Before attributing such gains to shared learning, quantify what pretraining adds to a matched target-only model, compare the result with tuned alternatives, and test that increment across the geographic and observation boundaries the application will cross.

## Data and code availability

Analysis source, design records and aggregate evidence are maintained in the dSDM repository (<https://github.com/BWBrook/dSDM>). Recorded input, source and output hashes connect the reported summaries to the accepted analyses. Raw responses, row-level predictions, camera identifiers and precise locations are held outside the tracked aggregate evidence.
