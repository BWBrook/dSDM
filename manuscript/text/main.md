# Testing the transferability of multi-species representations in species distribution models

<div id="authors"></div>

<!-- Review draft restructured on 5 October 2026. Numerical sources and analysis status are recorded in manuscript/evidence-ledger.md. -->

## Abstract

Multi-species pretraining may improve species distribution models by learning environmental representations that can be reused for individual species. We tested its contribution against matched target-only networks and tuned conventional learners in Tasmanian mammals, mainland mammals transferred to Tasmania, and south-eastern Australian birds. Primary comparisons and scoring rules were fixed before each outer assessment. Pretraining improved inner ROC AUC over target-only learning by 0.0087 in mainland mammals and 0.0058 in the primary bird panel. Those average gains did not persist geographically: the transfer contrasts were −0.0155 and −0.0004, respectively. Original Tasmanian models showed no consistent full-data AUC benefit. At full target-data availability, removing target pseudoabsences reduced mean AUC for all three tested methods and reversed the mean fine-tuning advantage over target-only learning in true skill statistic. A post hoc bird analysis showed that much of the absolute inner–outer AUC difference arose when evaluation changed from the whole domain to within regions; the inner pretraining advantage nevertheless remained. Gains over conventional models declined with recorded background richness where the outer diagnostic had sufficient support, and were negligible for the sparsest third of birds in inner testing. Fixed neural–conventional averages gained 0.0104 outer AUC with pretraining and 0.0096 without it. Small in-domain gains therefore did not establish transferable value from pretraining. Evaluation extent, observation support and matched comparators were essential to identifying that limit.

## Introduction

Multi-species data offer a possible way to improve predictions for species with limited observations. A model trained on many species may learn combinations of environmental variables that are useful for another species, reducing the amount of target-specific data needed to learn those relationships. Deep multi-species models have used shared environmental structure and spatial context to improve prediction [@botella2018; @deneu2021], and shared neural representations support species mapping at large scales [@cole2023]. Neural joint species models have also used latent structure to predict assemblages [@harris2015]. These precedents differ in inputs, response models and prediction objectives; they do not isolate the contribution of target-exclusive pretraining. Their practical value for a target species nevertheless depends on more than successful prediction by a deep network. The transferred representation must add useful information beyond what the same network can learn from the target data alone.

Three comparisons distinguish this benefit. First, pretraining should improve a matched target-only network. Second, the resulting model should compete with well-tuned conventional learners. Third, an improvement seen during model development should persist under the intended form of transfer [@wenger2012; @yates2018]. These comparisons answer different questions. A neural network may complement a tree-based model without benefiting from multi-species pretraining, and a model that predicts well within a sampled domain may gain little from pretraining when applied elsewhere. Broad SDM comparisons show that model rankings depend on the prediction task and performance measure [@norberg2019], while tuning and fitting choices materially affect presence-only benchmarks [@valavi2022]. Model selection can also exaggerate small development-set differences [@cawley2010].

Species observations create an additional difficulty. In presence–background modelling, the contrasting class contains unrecorded locations rather than verified absences. Differences between occurrence collection and background sampling can affect prediction [@phillips2009]. A multi-species representation might therefore encode environmental associations with recording as well as habitat. Restricting background to cells with other species records improves the support for treating a location as sampled, but it does not separate these processes. Studies that model observer bias or use checklist effort information show why the observation process needs explicit treatment [@warton2013; @johnston2021]. Discrimination against lightly recorded cells need not imply equally good discrimination among intensively recorded sites.

Evaluation design also changes the task. Spatial blocking should reflect the intended prediction setting [@roberts2017], and AUC depends on the geographic and environmental extent of the comparison [@lobo2008; @jimenezvalverde2012]. An AUC computed across a broad domain includes presence–background pairs from different regions. A regional AUC excludes those pairs. Comparing their absolute values without examining this distinction can conflate geographic transfer with a change in the discrimination problem.

We tested multi-species environmental representations across three complementary settings: a low-diversity, high-observed-prevalence Tasmanian mammal study; a mainland mammal model transferred to Tasmania; and a larger south-eastern Australian bird study with whole bioregions held out. We compared full fine-tuning with a matched target-only network, a frozen pretrained representation, scalar community controls and tuned conventional models. Target-data and auxiliary-species learning curves examined where gains occurred. The bird outer assessment then tested whether those gains persisted outside the retained regions. After an inner diagnostic implicated background recording richness, we froze a corresponding outer diagnostic before opening the held-out bird responses. We subsequently examined evaluation extent and a target-only ensemble as explicitly post hoc analyses.

## Methods

### Study designs and response definitions

The three settings differ in response construction, training data and transfer distance (Table 1). We treat them as complementary tests, not independent replicates of a single population effect. Some mammal species, observation sources and the Tasmanian evaluation geography are shared.

The original Tasmania study included *Thylogale billardierii*, *Notamacropus rufogriseus*, *Trichosurus vulpecula*, *Pseudocheirus peregrinus*, *Vombatus ursinus*, *Sarcophilus harrisii*, *Dasyurus maculatus* and *Dasyurus viverrinus*. Responses resolved 2001–2025 observations to species-by-0.01-degree grid cells. Presences, camera survey-supported absences and protocol-defined pseudoabsences formed the primary response; unknown species–cell combinations were masked. Each resolved row had equal weight. Camera nondetection was treated as survey-supported absence, not proof of ecological absence. The complete modelling substrate contained 12,711 species–cell rows, of which 11,039 were allocated to outer training. Five inner spatial folds supported model development. A pre-model frozen union of five held patches supplied 1,672 outer response rows across 606 geographic cells. These patches touch training areas and have no buffer, so this is an assessment of spatial interpolation within the defined domain.

The mainland-to-Tasmania experiment used 20 mainland response heads and 21 taxa as recording-support donors. For each of five shared targets, pretraining excluded the target and used the other 19 heads. Mainland target-group background required records of at least three other support species and a distance of at least 5 km from target presence. This construction was repeated within authorised training partitions. Tasmania contributed to neither training nor fitted transformations. The primary Tasmania evaluation used camera-supported cells; a secondary surface included all accepted occurrence and camera rows. The five targets were *D. maculatus*, *N. rufogriseus*, *P. peregrinus*, *T. vulpecula* and *V. ursinus*. The primary complete evaluation contained 4,077 species–cell rows.

The bird study used a source-defined universe of 505 recording-support donors and 259 eligible auxiliary-head or target candidates. Support rules yielded 157 focal targets, including a common panel of 73 eligible in all three held-out IBRA bioregions: Naracoorte Coastal Plain (NCP), South Eastern Highlands (SEH) and NSW North Coast (NNC). Before fitted outcomes were available, NCP was assigned to assess environmentally supported geographic transfer, SEH an environmental edge, and NNC a fragmented land-use mosaic. All three complete regions and their 50-km external buffers were excluded simultaneously from development. These designed roles organise the comparison; they do not isolate the effect of any one regional property. The regional secondary panels contained 88, 122 and 134 species, respectively. The accepted occurrence substrate contained 5,048,061 unique species–cell memberships. A positive response denoted a recorded presence. The primary background consisted of target-unrecorded cells with at least 10 other donor species recorded (X10); X20 was an evaluation sensitivity. Recording richness was not a predictor. These responses estimate discrimination of recorded presence from supported background, not occupancy or true prevalence.

Environmental predictors were encoded and transformed using the training side of each split. The original Tasmanian models used 28 raw predictors, represented by 77 encoded columns. The portable mainland and bird substrates used their separately frozen predictor schemas. Birds used 24 raw variables: the 19 bioclimatic variables, elevation mean and standard deviation, National Vegetation Information System major vegetation group, Australian Soil Classification order, and Australian Land Use and Management secondary class. Scaling and one-hot encoding produced 103 columns. Continuous scaling, categorical vocabularies and support handling were fitted within the appropriate training partitions. Bird inner folds comprised complete projected 50-km blocks allocated to five folds; the three outer regions were excluded from development under the frozen geographic rules.

The principal deep species distribution model (dSDM) was a residual multilayer perceptron with a shared environmental encoder and species-specific pretraining heads. Auxiliary species had equal total contribution to pretraining while retaining their within-species class imbalance. In Tasmania, the focal target was excluded from the seven auxiliary species, and auxiliary pseudoabsences were excluded. The bird experiment divided focal species into 10 blocks and excluded the entire focal block from auxiliary heads and auxiliary recording-richness construction. Target fine-tuning used the target's own response and its target-specific background rule.

The full-fine-tuning model updated the pretrained encoder and a new target head. Its central comparator used the same architecture, target data, optimisation and evaluation rules, with random initialisation. A frozen-trunk comparator fitted a target head without updating the pretrained encoder. Scalar community controls supplied a target-excluded pooled prediction as an additional covariate. Tasmania retained both linear and nonlinear scalar controls and legacy pooled pretraining; the bird experiment retained the linear scalar control. These controls test particular compressed representations and do not exhaust possible community summaries.

The original Tasmanian architecture had three residual blocks of width 128 and bottleneck width 64. The bird experiment selected one shared architecture from four predeclared candidates using the 73-species full-data development endpoint. The selected network had six residual blocks of width 256 and bottleneck width 128. All final neural comparisons used five matched seeds, with scores averaged before evaluation. The 73 bird selection species also contributed to the inner estimates; the other 84 targets did not select the architecture, but differed in support and composition. Neither panel was an independent outer assessment during development. The target-only network inherited an architecture selected for fine-tuning rather than receiving an independent architecture search. In the primary 73-species panel it nevertheless exceeded the tuned shallow ANN by mean AUC 0.0037 and trailed the selected conventional learner by 0.0024. These comparisons document a credible baseline without removing the selection asymmetry.

Bird residual networks used GELU activations, layer normalisation, AdamW at learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0.001, batch size 128, and at most 300 epochs. With held fold h, fold (h + 1) modulo 5 supplied early stopping, with patience 30 and minimum improvement 0.00001; discovery used the other three folds. The selected epoch was then replayed from the prescribed initialisation on all four non-held folds. Architecture screening used one seed; final comparisons used five matched seeds. Retained-full bird refits used the median selected epoch across the 25 matching fold-by-seed fits for each target and arm. Pretraining used the corresponding median for each excluded focal block, fixed before outer fitting. The shallow-ANN refit budgets were recovered and verified against accepted predictions before applying the same median rule. Supplementary Methods give the architecture candidates, conventional grids and seed rules.

original architecture and full predictor set. These inner diagnostics did not
authorise selection from the outer results.

The conventional comparison included an intercept null, elastic-net logistic regression, random forest, histogram gradient boosting and a shallow neural network. Hyperparameters and the best conventional family were selected within the retained data. The outer assessment retained those choices. All conventional families remained available in the original Tasmania assessment. Bird conventional candidate and family selection ranked equal-fold mean AUC first, pooled out-of-fold TSS second, then the fixed candidate or family identifier. The full-data selected bird benchmark contained 51 random forests, 66 histogram gradient boosters and 40 shallow ANNs. The bird study additionally evaluated the selected conventional learner with the linear community covariate. No response-driven predictor selection, spatial jitter augmentation or post-outer model tuning was used in these experiments.

Target learning curves used 25%, 50% and 100% of available target training data. Tasmania used three deterministic nested subset ladders at reduced fractions and one full-data endpoint. Birds used deterministic nested target fractions for the common 73 species, crossed with 20, 80 or the full eligible auxiliary pool; the remaining 84 targets received the full endpoint. Auxiliary-species number changes jointly with row volume and species composition, so this experiment does not identify an isolated causal effect of species richness. Final retained-full refits used epoch rules frozen from the inner fits. Model states, thresholds, selections and predictions were bound to recorded source and input hashes before the corresponding outer scoring stage.

Implementation used Python, PyTorch and scikit-learn, with dependencies fixed in a uv lockfile and source hashes recorded for each run. The bird environment fixed PyTorch 2.13.0, scikit-learn 1.9.0 and NumPy 2.5.1. Local checks and reporting ran on an Apple Silicon Mac mini; bird inner production used Kunanyi HPC and Nectar, and the retained-full outer campaign ran on the Linux Deep server. Process-level parallelism used controlled estimator thread counts. Run receipts preserve the source, environment and task inventories.

### Evaluation and analysis status

ROC AUC was the primary bird and mainland-transfer measure. AUC and true skill statistic (TSS) were co-primary in the original Tasmania protocol. Binary metrics applied thresholds selected from seed-averaged inner out-of-fold scores by the frozen maximum-TSS rule. Outer responses did not select thresholds. Balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation, sensitivity, specificity, class counts and, where specified, average precision were supplementary. Undefined patch metrics retained their class counts and were not filled by resampling.

The original Tasmania assessment reports pooled held-patch predictions and each patch separately for all species and endpoints. Its three principal contrasts are full fine-tuning minus target-only, minus the nonlinear scalar-control network, and minus the inner-selected conventional learner. Bird outer summaries weight species and the three regions equally in the common panel. Regional secondary panels remain separate. Seeds, spatial folds, nested subsets, shared auxiliary pools and species are not treated as independent ecological replicates. Summaries and positive-species counts are descriptive; near-zero mean contrasts do not establish equivalence. For the synthesis we report each species contrast, its mean, median and interquartile range (IQR), using linearly interpolated quartiles. We also report the range of means after omitting each species once, preserving paired model contrasts. This post hoc deletion check describes sensitivity to one member of the observed panel; it is neither a confidence interval nor a measure of uncertainty under joint ecological dependence (Table S1).

The original Tasmania protocol proposed a pseudoabsence-removal sensitivity. We implemented it across all eight species and seven endpoints by removing pseudoabsences from target discovery, stopping, replay and outer-fitting roles, while retaining every original evaluation row. Full-data target training lost 368 of 11,039 rows. Both residual networks and the primary-selected conventional candidate were refitted; auxiliary states, the encoded environmental substrate, neural architecture and conventional selections remained fixed. Target support masks and conventional encoders were refitted on the retained rows, and thresholds were reselected from the sensitivity out-of-fold predictions. We compared paired AUC, TSS, Matthews correlation and specificity by absence source, plus 24 full-data map pairs over the same 71,023 cells (Tables S5–S9). The training-only interpretation, threshold recalibration, comparator restriction and map summaries were specified after primary outer opening, so this implements a prespecified question without constituting a fully prospective confirmatory test.

For the two primary bird contrasts, we added a post hoc species-composition resampling check. Each of 100,000 replicates sampled 73 species with replacement, carrying their inner and three-region results for every method together. The same draw supplied both comparators and assessments. Central 95% resampling ranges are the linearly interpolated 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the replicate means (Fig. 1; Table S1d). They describe empirical reweighting of the observed panel, not confidence intervals, and exclude fitting, spatial-sampling, model-selection and population uncertainty. An inner-only width check resampled the ten frozen focal blocks, retaining every species within each sampled block and dividing the summed contrasts by the sampled species count (Table S1e). These unequal-sized computational groups share block-specific pretrained objects but are neither spatial nor taxonomic clusters; common folds, geography, architecture selection and overlapping auxiliary pools still create dependence between them.

Two bird recording-richness diagnostics were fixed before outer opening, following exploratory inner analysis. The first compared all focal presences with separate background bins containing 10–24, 25–99 or at least 100 other recorded donors. The second restricted both classes to cells with at least 100 other recorded donors. Support rules were applied unchanged. Common supported species within each region were used for comparisons across all three background bins.

After outer opening, we rescored the same bird inner predictions across the whole retained domain, within each named retained bioregion, and within each region-by-fold stratum. Regional pooled scoring required at least 30 cells of each class; region-by-fold scoring required at least 20. Eligible strata were averaged equally within species, then species equally. Whole-domain scoring included the domain complement outside the named regions, whereas the regional analyses excluded it. Region-by-fold scoring avoids mixing predictions from different fold-specific models. These analyses change the set and weighting of comparison pairs; they are not an experimental decomposition of geographic transfer loss.

A fixed equal-weight average of the fine-tuned network and selected conventional learner was specified before bird outer opening as an exploratory ensemble. We added an equal-weight target-only/conventional ensemble after opening, using the saved predictions. Neither ensemble used fitted outer weights or thresholds; comparisons are AUC-only. The latter specifically tests whether an ensemble benefit is also available without pretraining, but cannot establish that the two neural predictors contain identical information.

## Results

### The original Tasmanian inner comparison gave no consistent full-data advantage

Across the eight species and seven target-data endpoints, full fine-tuning exceeded target-only AUC in 35 of 56 species–endpoint comparisons, but the paired seed range crossed zero in 40. Reduced-data results were heterogeneous. At the full endpoint, median species-level differences in mean-fold AUC were −0.0027 against target-only, −0.0064 against the nonlinear scalar control and −0.0390 against the selected conventional learner. Equal-species mean AUC was 0.7133 for full fine-tuning, 0.7200 for target-only and 0.7631 for selected conventional learning. Conventional learning exceeded full fine-tuning in 53 of 56 comparisons and in all eight species at full target-data availability. The original Tasmanian inner result therefore does not support a general in-domain advantage of pretraining.

The initial signal from widening the network did not recur under its reserved
seed and endpoint checks. The aggregate TSS interaction was −0.0132, with
positive AUC interactions in only one of five confirmation endpoints and
three of eight species. Predictor reduction also failed its continuation
criteria: reduced-minus-full fine-tuning AUC was −0.0011 and TSS −0.0080.
These checks provided no basis to replace the original frozen model.

### The Tasmanian held patches gave contrasting AUC and TSS results

The original Tasmania outer assessment retained all eight species and all seven target-data endpoints. At full target data, fine-tuning attained equal-species mean AUC 0.8390, compared with 0.8537 for target-only, 0.8535 for the nonlinear scalar-control network and 0.8441 for selected conventional learning. Median paired AUC differences were −0.0120, −0.0144 and −0.0083, respectively; only one, two and three species favoured fine-tuning. The mean fine-tuning minus target-only difference was −0.0147 (Fig. 1).

The co-primary TSS comparison differed. At the frozen inner thresholds, median fine-tuning minus target-only TSS was +0.0205, with five positive species, two negative and one tied; the mean difference was +0.0477. Against the nonlinear scalar control, median TSS difference was +0.0165 with five positive species. Against conventional learning, median TSS difference was −0.0262, while the mean was +0.0260 and four species were positive. These differences between ranking, thresholded performance and mean versus median preclude a single claim of consistent improvement. Of the 40 species-by-patch combinations, 28 had both response classes, eight had only one class and four had no rows; undefined patch metrics remained missing. Pooled metrics were defined for every species. The high pooled AUC describes these touching held patches and their response composition, not performance in an independent region.

AUC ranks all presence–negative pairs, whereas TSS uses a single threshold transferred from inner out-of-fold predictions to retained-full model scores. Lower AUC can coexist with higher TSS if ROC curves cross or the frozen threshold falls at a more favourable operating point. The current results do not distinguish these explanations; no outer threshold was optimised to revise the co-primary assessment.

### Pseudoabsence removal changed performance and mapped rankings

Removing pseudoabsences from target training reduced full-data mean held-patch AUC to 0.7704 for target-only learning, 0.7657 for fine-tuning and 0.7728 for the selected conventional learner (Table S6). The mean fine-tuning minus target-only AUC deficit narrowed from −0.0147 to −0.0047, but its TSS contrast changed from +0.0477 to −0.0182 and its Matthews correlation contrast from +0.0290 to −0.0104. Against conventional learning, fine-tuning retained lower AUC (−0.0071) but higher TSS (+0.0393) and Matthews correlation (+0.0527). Across the seven separate endpoints, the sensitivity mean fine-tuning minus target-only AUC contrast was negative at six, and its TSS contrast was negative at all seven (Table S7).

The full-data species-level AUC losses underlying these means were concentrated in *V. ursinus* and *D. maculatus*: reductions across the three methods were 0.419–0.463 and 0.068–0.204, respectively. Their held negative classes contained 18/25 and 29/50 pseudoabsences. For *V. ursinus*, pseudoabsence true negatives fell from 16, 15 and 15 to zero for target-only, fine-tuning and conventional learning, respectively. Both neural models still correctly classified all seven survey-supported absences; conventional learning improved from three to seven (Table S9). For *D. maculatus*, survey-absence specificity improved for all three methods while pseudoabsence specificity declined. The large aggregate declines therefore conceal different responses to the two negative-class sources.

For the seven species whose training data changed, full-data map correlations ranged from 0.584 to 0.983 and retention of the original top-decile cells from 31.4% to 85.5% (Table S8). Held discrimination and map stability did not change together: *D. viverrinus* lost only 0.003–0.011 AUC but retained 50.2–63.3% of its original top-decile cells across methods. *T. billardierii*, which had no target pseudoabsences to remove, reproduced its primary predictions, thresholds and maps exactly. These are changes in model scores and rankings over the fixed structural domain, not observed changes in occupancy or range.

### Small mainland inner gains did not persist in the average Tasmania transfer result

For the same five targets in strict mainland inner cross-validation, fine-tuning attained mean-fold AUC 0.9091, compared with 0.9004 for target-only and 0.9052 for selected conventional learning. The paired mean gains were +0.0087 (four of five species positive) and +0.00395 (five of five). Thus the mainland experiment, like the birds, had a small in-domain advantage to test geographically (Fig. 1).

On the five-species primary camera-supported Tasmania evaluation, full fine-tuning attained mean AUC 0.5560, compared with 0.5715 for target-only, 0.5787 for the linear scalar-control network and 0.5837 for the selected conventional learner. Full fine-tuning exceeded target-only for three species and conventional learning for one, but was the best model for none. Its mean TSS was −0.0119 and mean Matthews correlation was −0.0262. The secondary all-row evaluation gave the same direction of comparison. These are five-species descriptive results under a joint geographic and observation-domain change. The narrower within-island extent and camera-supported negative class also change the discrimination task, so the large absolute decline from mainland AUC cannot be assigned to geographic distance alone. The transfer contrast against target-only had a positive median (+0.0269) despite its negative mean; deleting a single species moved the mean between −0.0435 and +0.0169 (Table S1). This limits how broadly the five-species average can be interpreted.

### Bird pretraining gains were small within the retained domain

For the common 73 bird species at full target and auxiliary data, full fine-tuning attained inner mean-fold AUC 0.8316, compared with 0.8258 for target-only and 0.8282 for conventional learning. The mean gains were +0.0058 and +0.0034, with positive differences for 72 and 67 species, respectively. Among the other 84 targets, the corresponding mean gains were +0.0034 and +0.0009. These panels have different support and composition: median retained presence counts were 14,063 and 3,167.5. Their contrast does not isolate selection optimism or naturally sparse-species benefit.

Within the common panel, fine-tuning minus conventional AUC was +0.0069, +0.0053 and +0.0034 at 25%, 50% and 100% target data. A post hoc comparison showed that fine-tuning with 50% of target data exceeded conventional learning with 100% for 56 of 73 species, with a mean difference of +0.0016. This is data efficiency under subsampling of these species. At the primary panel’s median of 14,063 retained presences, one quarter of the four-fifths training share still represents about 2,800 presences. In a separate post hoc division by record count, the sparsest third of all targets comprised 53 species with 224–3,649 retained presence cells. Their full-data fine-tuning gain over conventional learning averaged only +0.0006, with 35 of 53 species positive. Fine-tuning and the frozen trunk differed by less than 0.000001 in their mean AUC. The corresponding matched-network pretraining gain was +0.0041. Pretraining therefore improved on this network baseline for sparse targets but supplied little average practical gain over tuned conventional models.

Frozen-trunk minus target-only AUC increased from −0.0044 with 20 auxiliary species to +0.0016 with 80 and +0.0022 with the full pool. All 73 species improved from the smallest to the full pool, although only 57 exceeded target-only at the full endpoint. This was a clearer scaling pattern than for full fine-tuning, whose mean gain peaked at 80 auxiliary species. The linear scalar control supplied negligible average incremental discrimination under its tested implementation.

### The average pretraining advantage did not persist across held-out bird regions

The outer primary estimate gave mean AUC 0.6827 for full fine-tuning and 0.6831 for target-only. Their mean difference was −0.0004, with 34 of 73 species positive. Regional mean differences were +0.0064 in NCP, −0.0040 in SEH and −0.0035 in NNC. Fine-tuning minus conventional learning averaged +0.0028, with 39 species positive, but again changed sign: +0.0078, −0.0071 and +0.0076 in NCP, SEH and NNC. Fine-tuning minus the linear scalar-control network was −0.0001 overall, and frozen trunk minus target-only was −0.0018. The X20 sensitivity preserved the broad pattern. The only positive regional mean pretraining contrast occurred in NCP, the region assigned the supported-transfer role. SEH also had the lowest fine-tuning minus conventional mean, consistent with a qualitative expectation recorded before opening. These are three designed regional cases, not a replicated test of the causal effect of environmental support.

Thresholded results did not exactly parallel the AUC contrasts: macro TSS was 0.2018 for fine-tuning and 0.1920 for target-only at the frozen inner thresholds. The score scales were not recalibrated on outer data. This difference is compatible with a near-zero mean ranking advantage because AUC and fixed-threshold discrimination measure different properties.

The negative SEH mean concealed a positive median fine-tuning minus target-only contrast (+0.0021), with 41 of 73 species positive. The most negative differences were −0.1498 for *Coracina papuensis* (170 held-region presences), −0.0900 for *Smicrornis brevirostris* (2,235) and −0.0778 for *Artamus cyanopterus* (3,157). Thus the negative tail was not confined to species with very few presences. Fine-tuning minus conventional learning in SEH had median −0.0009 and 36 positive species. All species were retained in these summaries.

Both inner mean contrasts remained positive across their central 95% species-composition resampling ranges: +0.0050 to +0.0066 against target-only and +0.0029 to +0.0039 against conventional learning. Both outer ranges crossed zero: −0.0041 to +0.0034 and −0.0017 to +0.0072, respectively (Fig. 1; Table S1d). The inner focal-block check narrowed the target-only range and widened the conventional range (Table S1e). These patterns describe stability under the specified reweighting exercises, not statistical significance.

### Within-region scoring changed absolute AUC but retained the inner pretraining advantage

The post hoc extent check reproduced the large difference between broad-domain and regional discrimination using unchanged inner predictions (Fig. 2a). Pooled whole-domain AUC for fine-tuning was 0.8327; averaging its AUC within named retained regions gave 0.6911, and averaging within region-by-fold strata gave 0.6897. The latter was close to the held-region mean of 0.6827. However, regional scoring excluded 360,625 retained domain-complement cells and changed the comparison pairs and their weighting. The remaining 0.0070 difference is therefore descriptive, not an isolated geographic penalty.

The pretraining contrast remained positive under both regional aggregations. Fine-tuning minus target-only AUC averaged +0.0110 within pooled regions and +0.0072 within region-by-fold strata, with 56 and 68 of 73 species positive, respectively. These calculations used 696 eligible species-by-region and 2,650 species-by-region-by-fold strata. They support an inner pretraining advantage even when evaluation excludes cross-region ranking pairs; that average advantage was absent from the held-region estimate.

### Most of the outer ensemble gain was available without pretraining

The prospectively specified exploratory fine-tuning/conventional average attained outer AUC 0.6903, gaining +0.0104 over conventional learning and +0.0076 over fine-tuning. The post hoc target-only/conventional average attained 0.6896 and gained +0.0096 over conventional learning (Fig. 2b). Both ensemble gains were positive in each region. Across species after equal-region averaging, 60 of 73 improved on conventional learning with the fine-tuned ensemble and 64 with the target-only ensemble.

The two ensembles differed by only +0.0007 on average in favour of pretraining, with median +0.00009 and 37 of 73 species positive. The regional differences were +0.0038 in NCP, −0.0023 in SEH and +0.0007 in NNC. For comparison, their inner mean-fold gains over conventional learning were +0.0058 and +0.0031. The outer ensemble benefit was therefore largely available from the target-only network, although this descriptive comparison does not establish equivalence or identical predictive information.

### Recording-richness dependence persisted where support allowed assessment

The exploratory inner analysis that motivated this diagnostic showed a decline in fine-tuning minus conventional AUC across the 10–24, 25–99 and 100+ background bins: +0.0050, +0.0019 and −0.0039 for the same 59 supported primary targets. The matched-network contrast across 142 supported targets likewise declined from +0.0053 to +0.0034 and −0.0021. An exact background-share-weighted decomposition across all 73 primary targets assigned contributions of +0.002605, +0.000832 and −0.000047 to the three bins, recovering the full +0.003390 conventional contrast. Thus most of that net gain arose from comparisons against the least richly recorded background. These discoveries used retained inner predictions; the subsequent outer diagnostic was specified before opening (Table S2).

For the same supported species across background bins, fine-tuning minus conventional AUC declined with recorded richness. In SEH, the means were −0.0038, −0.0140 and −0.0283 across the 10–24, 25–99 and 100+ bins (38 species). In NNC they were +0.0148, +0.0060 and −0.0259 (55 species). NCP had no species with the required background support in the highest bin, so no three-region replication estimate was available.

Restricting both classes to 100+ other recorded donors also favoured conventional learning on average: the fine-tuning contrast was −0.0373 in SEH (34 species) and −0.0079 in NNC (53 species). This separate diagnostic likewise lacked support in NCP. High-richness background represented only 0.28%, 0.61% and 2.77% of the mean species-level X10 background share in NCP, SEH and NNC. Large differences within that subset can therefore coexist with much smaller overall differences. Neither diagnostic identifies a causal effect of survey effort or what information the encoder used.

## Discussion

In both settings with small average in-domain gains—mainland mammals and birds—the matched-network advantage was absent from the intended geographic transfer mean. The mainland result was heterogeneous across five species, whereas the bird mean was close to zero across 73. Neither constitutes evidence of uniform transfer failure. The original Tasmanian inner experiment was already weak or negative at full target-data availability, and its held-patch AUC favoured target-only learning for seven of eight species. TSS more often favoured fine-tuning in those patches, so the co-primary metrics gave different answers. The evidence does not support a claim that pretraining consistently improves in-domain performance across all cases, or that its bird development-set gain establishes a transferable ecological representation.

The controlled comparison is crucial. Fine-tuning remained slightly ahead of selected conventional learning in the bird outer mean, and neural–conventional averaging could improve predictions. Neither observation alone demonstrates a pretraining benefit. The target-only network tests the contribution of pretraining under the same architecture and target-learning design. The conventional comparator instead measures practical performance against a tuned panel of alternative learners, including shallow neural networks. A method can pass one comparison while failing the other. Combining predictions has a long history in SDM [@araujo2007], but its value depends on the constituent learners and how they are fitted [@valavi2022]. Here most of the fixed ensemble gain was also available without auxiliary pretraining.

Absolute AUC also needs context. Restricting the retained predictions to within-region comparisons changes the discrimination task without moving any fitted model into a new region. Broad-domain AUC includes cross-region pairs that regional AUC omits, and the domain complement contributes to only the broad-domain analysis. The extent check therefore helps explain why the unqualified inner–outer AUC gap is an unsuitable measure of transfer loss. It does not identify a remaining “true geographic penalty”: retained and held-out regions differ in composition and support, while the predictions come from fold-specific and retained-full models, respectively. The disappearance of the matched pretraining advantage is a more focused result than the decline in absolute AUC. Such task-dependent rankings accord with broader multi-model evaluations [@norberg2019].

The recording-richness diagnostics further constrain interpretation. A gain concentrated against lightly recorded background can arise from several combinations of environmental, recording and biological structure. Our analysis does not separate those explanations. The outer replication in SEH and NNC strengthens the evidence for this dependence, while NCP's lack of high-richness support limits its geographic generality. The pretrained representation may reflect region-specific environmental, community or recording structure, but the present design cannot identify their contributions. Fine-tuning could also amplify features specific to the retained domain. Feature distortion under fine-tuning has been demonstrated in computer-vision transfer tasks [@kumar2022]; it remains a hypothesis here, with different inputs, losses and ecological shifts.

Several boundaries matter. Architecture selection used the same bird development panel that supplied its inner estimates. Stable leave-block-out architecture rankings do not remove this selection dependence. The auxiliary-size experiment changes species composition and record volume together. Subsampling widespread targets does not reproduce every difficulty faced by rare taxa. The original Tasmanian patches assess interpolation, whereas the mainland-to-Tasmania experiment changes geography and response source together. Across all settings, spatial dependence and shared species or auxiliary pools preclude interpreting the number of positive contrasts as independent replication. A near-zero mean is a failure to demonstrate average benefit under these designs, not proof of equivalence or a limit on every possible architecture.

The completed Tasmania sensitivity shows that both the high primary AUC and the mapped rankings can depend on the target negative-class construction. Removing a relatively small number of training pseudoabsences produced large held-panel losses for two species, while specificity for their survey-supported absences was preserved or improved. This is a source-dependent tradeoff within an unchanged evaluation mixture, not evidence that the constructed pseudoabsences are ecologically correct or incorrect. Filtering changes class balance, effective spatial support, stopping and calibration together; the separately reselected thresholds add another distinction from a fixed-threshold perturbation. The full-data TSS advantage of fine-tuning over target-only learning did not survive this sensitivity, although contrasts against conventional learning remained metric-dependent. Neither the primary maps nor the refitted maps have independent occupancy validation.

The results point to a practical standard for evaluating multi-species pretraining: retain a matched target-only network, tune credible conventional comparators, test the intended geographic transfer, and state precisely what the negative class represents. In the present experiments, a larger auxiliary pool improved some retained-domain representations, but did not establish a general regional advantage from pretraining. The next priority is independent, observation-aware evaluation [@warton2013; @johnston2021]. Known-truth simulations could help distinguish ecological and recording mechanisms under explicit assumptions. Richer habitat inputs are a second priority: spatial environmental context can improve deep SDMs [@deneu2021], whereas the current networks received cell-level summaries. These extensions need a newly fixed selection and evaluation design; the opened holdouts cannot supply untouched confirmation for changes motivated by their results.
