# Does learning from other species improve species distribution models in new regions?

<div id="authors"></div>

<!-- Working manuscript, 18 October 2026. Accepted analyses are unchanged. Drafting and review are recorded in docs/plan/ManuscriptDrafting-20261018.md. -->

## Abstract

1. Learning from other species could help distribution models predict beyond the places where a target species has been recorded. The appeal is to reuse environmental information that a model of the target alone would miss. We tested whether this benefit persists in new regions, distinguishing the contribution of multi-species pretraining from the performance of the neural model itself.

2. Three experiments used cell-level environmental predictors: eight Tasmanian mammals, five mainland mammals transferred to Tasmania, and 157 south-eastern Australian birds. The primary bird panel comprised 73 species in three whole bioregions withheld with 50-km buffers. Each pretrained, fine-tuned network was compared with the same architecture trained only on the target and with tuned conventional models. Primary models and scoring rules were fixed before examining withheld responses.

3. Pretraining improved development discrimination, measured by area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC), by less than 0.01 in mainland mammals and birds. Neither retained a positive mean pretraining gain in the new regions. In withheld bird regions, fine-tuning exceeded conventional models by 0.0028 AUC; the target-only network exceeded them by 0.0032. Most of the improvement from averaging neural and conventional predictions was also available without pretraining. Post hoc analyses showed that regional scoring alone sharply reduced absolute AUC, while restricting Tasmanian negatives to survey-supported nondetections reversed the mean AUC ranking of fine-tuned and conventional models.

4. With these tabular predictors, the small average neural advantage in the withheld bird regions was available without pretraining. A successful pretrained model can therefore give a misleading impression of what learning across species contributed. That contribution needs to be measured against a matched target-only model in the geographic and observation settings where predictions will be used.

**Keywords:** geographic transfer; multi-species learning; observation process; presence–background models; spatial validation; transfer learning.

## Introduction

Conservation decisions require predictions in places where species records are incomplete. Transferring a distribution model from better-sampled areas offers a way to fill those gaps, provided its predictions remain useful in the new setting [@yates2018]. Learning from other species could help. Species share aspects of their responses to climate and habitat, so observations of one species can contain information relevant to another [@ovaskainen2025]. Multi-species pretraining uses this opportunity by first fitting a neural network to other species, then adapting it to the focal target. The attraction is especially clear when target records are scarce: the model can draw on information that would be unavailable if each species were fitted in isolation.

Sharing information among species can improve ecological prediction, although gains depend on the task and on which species are modelled [@norberg2019]. Multi-species embeddings and neural joint species models learn common structure in environmental responses [@harris2015; @chen2017], while deep distribution models exploit environmental and spatial context from regional to global scales [@botella2018; @deneu2021; @cole2023]. Recent applications model plant communities [@brun2024], transfer presence-only representations to abundance prediction [@bourel2025], and use common species to inform predictions for rare ones [@ovaskainen2025]. Representations learned without the evaluation species can also support few-shot range estimation [@lange2025], and sequential deep learning has been assessed against external plant records across geographic scales [@santos2026]. These advances give good reason to test whether shared learning improves predictions in new regions.

A good pretrained model, however, does not by itself tell us how much sharing information helped. A network with the same architecture might predict just as well after training only on the focal species. Comparing those two networks measures what pretraining adds; comparing them with a tuned tree ensemble or another conventional SDM assesses whether either improves on a credible predictive benchmark. Both comparisons are needed. A gain from pretraining may still leave the neural model behind a conventional alternative, while a neural advantage may already be available without pretraining. Same-architecture and no-transfer controls have precedents [@chen2017; @bourel2025]. Among the close studies above, we found no test combining target-excluded auxiliary pretraining, a matched target-only network and whole-region geographic exclusion. This combination tests whether learning from other species helps a focal species where the model has not been developed.

Geographic evaluation matters because a model that predicts withheld locations within a sampled domain faces a different task from one predicting a whole new region [@wenger2012; @yates2018]. Spatial cross-validation must represent that intended use [@roberts2017]. Interpreting the resulting performance also requires care. AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen recorded presence receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative or background location. Across a large domain, some of these pairs span very different environments; within a region, those broad contrasts disappear. A model's AUC can fall simply because the comparison covers a smaller area, even when its predictions have not changed [@lobo2008; @jimenezvalverde2012]. The apparent loss of skill on transfer can therefore mix a change in the model's predictions with a change in the task used to judge them.

The observations used to assess a prediction are just as consequential. Many occurrence datasets tell us where a species was recorded but leave its absence uncertain. Models fitted to these data distinguish records from background locations, learning environmental associations with recording as well as with habitat [@phillips2009; @warton2013]. Records of other species provide evidence that a cell was sampled, but the focal species may still have been overlooked. Because several species can share a recording process, pretraining might also share information about where observers looked. Comparing performance against well-recorded background and survey-supported nondetections tests how much the model ranking depends on the evidence used to define a negative observation.

Here we ask whether the predictive benefit of multi-species pretraining survives transfer to new regions. We compare matched target-only networks and tuned conventional SDMs across three Australian mammal and bird experiments, using cell-level climate and habitat summaries as inputs. This keeps the environmental information comparable across methods and focuses the test on tabular representations. The largest experiment includes 157 bird targets, with 73 evaluated in three whole bioregions kept outside model development. Target-data and auxiliary-species learning curves examine when sharing information is most useful. We then use evaluation extent, recording richness, model averaging and negative-class comparisons to interpret the gains. The aim is to estimate what learning from other species contributes to predictions in new places, and how that judgement depends on the observations used to assess them.

## Methods

### Ecological settings and prediction boundaries

We bring together three separately specified experiments addressing different prediction tasks (Table 1). They share some mammal taxa, observation sources and Tasmanian geography, so we synthesise them descriptively. Throughout, *development* denotes evaluation within the training domain using spatial folds; *outer assessment* denotes evaluation in the separately withheld geography.

Occurrence records for 2001–2025 were accessed through the Atlas of Living Australia [@belbin2021ala], with private camera-survey evidence contributing to the Tasmanian responses. Acquisition dates, taxonomic authorities, environmental products and unresolved source metadata are listed in Supplementary Methods S4. The mainland and bird experiments shared a versioned environmental substrate, including WorldClim 2.1 climate summaries [@fick2017worldclim]; the original Tasmania experiment used a separate legacy stack.

The original Tasmania experiment modelled eight mammal species on a 0.01-degree grid, combining recorded presences, camera survey-supported nondetections and constructed pseudoabsences. Unknown species–cell combinations were masked. Its 12,711 resolved response rows included 11,039 for development and 1,672 in five patches fixed before modelling. The patches contained 606 geographic cells, touched training areas and had no external buffer. They assess interpolation within Tasmania.

The mainland experiment pretrained on 19 auxiliary mammal species for each of five targets. Mainland background cells required records of at least three other support species and separation of at least 5 km from a target presence. Tasmania supplied neither training responses nor fitted transformations. The primary transfer assessment used 4,077 camera-supported species–cell rows in Tasmania; an all-source assessment was secondary. This transfer changes observation source as well as geography.

The bird experiment used records of 505 taxa to identify sampled cells; we call these taxa the recording-support set. Of the species considered for modelling, 259 were eligible as auxiliary or target candidates. The dataset contained 5,048,061 unique species–cell memberships. Support criteria admitted 157 targets, of which 73 could be evaluated in every withheld region. All three regions and their 50-km external buffers were excluded simultaneously from development. We selected these contrasting prediction settings before model outcomes were available (Table 1). The regional secondary panels contained 88, 122 and 134 targets in Naracoorte Coastal Plain, South Eastern Highlands and NSW North Coast, respectively.

Bird positives were recorded presences. Primary background cells had no focal record and records of at least ten other species from the support set; we also evaluated a stricter requirement of 20 such species. Recording richness was not a predictor. Across the experiments, models discriminate recorded presence from the specified negative observations. Neither background nor camera nondetection establishes ecological absence, so the predictions are not occupancy or prevalence estimates.

### Isolating the contribution of pretraining

All networks received cell-level environmental summaries. The original Tasmania schema had 28 raw predictors and 77 encoded columns. Birds used 24 variables: 19 bioclimatic variables, elevation mean and standard deviation, vegetation group, soil order and land-use class, yielding 103 encoded columns. The mainland experiment used its separately fixed portable schema. Scaling and categorical encoding were fitted on the training side of each partition. Bird development folds kept projected 50-km spatial blocks intact.

The deep species distribution model was a residual multilayer perceptron. A shared *encoder* transformed environmental inputs into features used by separate species prediction heads during pretraining. Auxiliary species contributed equal total weight while retaining their own class imbalance. The focal target was excluded from auxiliary learning. In birds, targets were grouped into ten computational blocks and the entire focal block was excluded from auxiliary heads and auxiliary recording-richness construction. Geographic exclusions applied to auxiliary learning as well as target fitting.

After pretraining, *fine-tuning* fitted a new target head and updated the shared encoder using the target's data. The central *target-only* control used the same architecture, target responses, optimisation and evaluation rules, starting from random weights. Their paired performance difference measures the gain from pretraining. A frozen-encoder control fitted only a target head, testing whether the shared features were useful without further adaptation. Scalar controls supplied a target-excluded pooled community prediction as one additional covariate.

Five matched seeds were used for final neural comparisons, with predictions averaged before scoring. For birds, one shared architecture was selected from four fixed candidates using fine-tuning performance on the common 73-species development panel. The chosen network had six residual blocks of width 256 and a bottleneck of width 128. The target-only control inherited that architecture without a separate search. This asymmetry conditions the comparison on an architecture selected for fine-tuning and makes the development estimates vulnerable to selection optimism [@cawley2010]. Target-only nevertheless exceeded a separately tuned shallow neural network by mean development AUC 0.0037, while trailing the selected conventional model by 0.0024.

Conventional candidates comprised an intercept model, elastic-net logistic regression, random forest, histogram gradient boosting and a shallow neural network. Hyperparameters and the preferred family were selected within development data and fixed for outer prediction. Early stopping, replay and retained-data refitting likewise used development information only. No outer response selected a model, epoch, ensemble weight or threshold. Model specifications, grids and software versions are given in Supplementary Methods and Table S3.

Target-data curves used 25%, 50% and 100% of available training data. Tasmania used three nested subset ladders at reduced fractions, producing seven endpoints. Birds crossed the common panel's target fractions with 20, 80 or the full eligible auxiliary-species pool. Larger pools included both more records and a different mixture of species. These curves test reduced data availability for the same targets; the least-recorded species were assessed separately. Conventional models were selected separately at each data fraction, and their random seeds were not paired with the neural seeds. Comparisons between data fractions are therefore descriptive assessments of the fitted pipelines.

### Performance and diagnostic analyses

ROC AUC was primary for mainland transfer and birds. AUC and true skill statistic (TSS; sensitivity plus specificity minus one) were co-primary in Tasmania. TSS used thresholds chosen by a fixed maximum-TSS rule from development out-of-fold predictions, then held fixed for outer evaluation. Supplementary metrics included Matthews correlation and source-specific specificity. AUC assesses score ranking; TSS assesses performance at one operating threshold, so their conclusions need not coincide.

Primary bird summaries average the three regional AUCs within each species, then weight species equally. Development AUC averages folds within species. We report paired species contrasts, means, medians and positive-species counts, retaining every species. Because species share geography and auxiliary models, these summaries are descriptive. Post hoc single-species deletion and paired species-composition resampling assess sensitivity to the observed panel's composition (Table S1). The resampling ranges in Fig. 1 cover this source of variation only; they are not confidence intervals and omit uncertainty from fitting, spatial sampling and selection.

An exploratory development analysis compared bird discrimination against background containing records of 10–24, 25–99 or at least 100 other species from the support set. All focal presences were reused across the three bins. We specified the corresponding withheld-region diagnostic and common-species support rules before examining those regions' responses. A second diagnostic restricted both classes to cells with at least 100 other recorded species from the set. Together they test how model comparisons depend on recording support.

Two further bird analyses were added after the withheld-region results were available. First, we recomputed AUC from unchanged development predictions across the whole retained domain, within named retained bioregions, and within region-by-fold strata. Regional and region-by-fold scoring required at least 30 and 20 cells per class, respectively, and averaged eligible strata equally within species. Regional scoring omits cells outside the named regions and changes the comparison pairs and weights. Second, we compared a target-only/conventional equal-weight ensemble with the fine-tuned/conventional equal-weight ensemble specified beforehand as exploratory. Both use saved scores without fitted ensemble weights.

The original Tasmania protocol proposed removing pseudoabsences from target training. The implementation across all eight species and seven endpoints was specified after the primary withheld-patch results were available. It removed 368 full-data training rows while retaining the original evaluation panel, architecture, pretrained auxiliary states and selected conventional candidates. Target models were refitted and thresholds reselected from their development predictions. A subsequent post hoc evaluation retained all 1,300 withheld presences and 242 survey-supported negatives, excluding 130 withheld pseudoabsences. It reused the saved predictions and thresholds from both training regimes, allowing training changes and evaluation changes to be examined separately. Details, source counts and map comparisons are in Tables S5–S10.

## Results

### Development gains from pretraining did not persist on average in new regions

Pretraining modestly improved predictions within the training domain (Fig. 1). For the five mainland mammals, fine-tuning gained +0.0087 mean AUC over the target-only network, with gains for four species, and +0.0040 over conventional models, with gains for all five. For the 73 birds, the mean gains were +0.0058 over target-only and +0.0034 over conventional models, positive for 72 and 67 species, respectively. Absolute AUCs for all three experiments are reported in Table S1f.

Neither experiment retained a positive mean gain from pretraining in the new regions. For mainland mammals transferred to Tasmania, fine-tuning trailed the target-only network (mean AUC difference −0.0155), although three of five species benefited and the median difference was +0.0269. Leave-one-species-out means ranged from −0.0435 to +0.0169, reflecting the variation among this small set of targets.

Across the three withheld bird regions, mean AUC was 0.6827 for fine-tuning and 0.6831 for target-only: a paired difference of −0.0004, positive for 34 of 73 species. The regional gains were +0.0064 in NCP, −0.0040 in SEH and −0.0035 in NNC. The sole positive regional mean occurred in the region chosen for environmentally supported transfer. Against conventional models, fine-tuning averaged +0.0028 overall, with regional contrasts of +0.0078, −0.0071 and +0.0076. Even within SEH, a negative mean pretraining gain coexisted with a positive median of +0.0021 and 41 positive species: losses in some species outweighed gains in the majority. The target-only network also exceeded conventional models in the withheld regions, by +0.0032 AUC on average. The small average advantage over conventional models was therefore available without pretraining.

The development increments remained positive under the species-resampling checks, whereas both outer contrast ranges crossed zero (Fig. 1; Table S1). Fixed-threshold bird TSS also differed from the AUC comparison: 0.2018 for fine-tuning versus 0.1920 for target-only.

The original Tasmania experiment had no consistent development AUC advantage from pretraining: full-data mean contrasts were −0.0068 against target-only and −0.0498 against conventional models. In the withheld patches, fine-tuning also trailed target-only by mean AUC (−0.0147) while exceeding it by co-primary TSS (+0.0477). Only one of eight species had a positive AUC contrast, compared with five positive TSS contrasts and one tie. Ranking and fixed-threshold performance thus gave different assessments of benefit.

Within the bird development data, reducing target records increased fine-tuning's advantage over conventional models: +0.0069 at 25%, +0.0053 at 50% and +0.0034 at full data. For scale, the conventional pipeline gained +0.0037 mean AUC when its target data doubled from 50% to 100% (0.8245 to 0.8282). A post hoc comparison found that half-data fine-tuning exceeded full-data conventional models by +0.0016 on average. However, the primary panel's median 14,063 retained presences meant that the quarter-data fits still used roughly 2,800 presences. In the least-recorded third of all 157 targets—53 species with 224–3,649 presence cells—the full-data advantage was only +0.0006 over conventional models, despite a +0.0041 increment over target-only. Evidence for data efficiency in the main panel therefore did not establish a comparable practical benefit for the least-recorded species.

Auxiliary learning did supply reusable development information: the frozen-encoder contrast with target-only rose from −0.0044 with 20 auxiliary species to +0.0022 with the full pool, with all 73 targets improving. Full fine-tuning instead peaked at 80 auxiliary species; the pool changes species composition and record volume together.

### Evaluation extent and model averaging change the interpretation of performance

The absolute bird AUC decline was strongly dependent on the comparison extent (Fig. 2a). Using unchanged development predictions, fine-tuned AUC was 0.8327 across the whole retained domain, 0.6911 when averaged within named regions and 0.6897 within region-by-fold strata. The last value was close to the withheld-region mean of 0.6827. Regional scoring excluded 360,625 domain-complement cells and changed which presence–background pairs contributed to AUC.

The pretraining increment nevertheless remained positive under both regional aggregations: +0.0110 within regions and +0.0072 within region-by-fold strata, positive for 56 and 68 of 73 species, respectively. Thus broad-domain ranking pairs alone did not account for the development advantage that was absent, on average, in the withheld regions.

Model averaging gave a larger improvement (Fig. 2b). The fine-tuned/conventional ensemble reached withheld-region AUC 0.6903, an increase of +0.0104 over conventional models, positive for 60 of 73 species. The post hoc target-only/conventional ensemble reached 0.6896, an increase of +0.0096, positive for 64 species. The two ensembles differed by only +0.0007 in the mean, with the pretrained ensemble favoured for 37 species. Most of the average ensemble gain was available from a network trained on the target species alone.

### The definition of negative observations changed practical rankings

Fine-tuning's advantage over conventional models declined as background recording richness increased (Fig. 3; Table S2). For the same 59 primary bird species evaluated in every bin, the mean contrast fell from +0.0050 against cells with 10–24 other recorded species to +0.0019 for 25–99 and −0.0039 for at least 100. The contrasts with the matched target-only networks followed the same progression: +0.0075, +0.0039 and −0.0043.

The prospectively fixed outer diagnostic showed a similar decline where all three bins were supported. For the common 38-species SEH panel, conventional-model contrasts were −0.0038, −0.0140 and −0.0283; for the common 55-species NNC panel, they were +0.0148, +0.0060 and −0.0259. NCP had no species supported in all three bins and supplies no corresponding comparison. Restricting both classes to high-richness cells also gave negative mean conventional-model contrasts in SEH and NNC (−0.0373 and −0.0079).

High-richness background was uncommon: the mean within-species shares were 0.28%, 0.61% and 2.77% in NCP, SEH and NNC, respectively. The largest mean disadvantages for fine-tuning occurred in this small, well-recorded part of the evaluation data.

The Tasmania experiment provides a complementary comparison of constructed and survey-supported negatives. Removing constructed pseudoabsences from target training initially appeared detrimental: mean AUC on the unchanged mixed evaluation panel fell for all three methods (Table 2a). The interpretation reversed when evaluation was restricted to survey-supported negatives. On this post hoc panel, pseudoabsence removal raised mean AUC from 0.8352 to 0.8572 for fine-tuning, from 0.8403 to 0.8587 for target-only and from 0.8198 to 0.8385 for conventional models. The training change's apparent benefit therefore depended on which negative observations defined the test.

The comparator mattered as well. Fine-tuning remained below target-only in mean AUC under both training regimes and both evaluation panels. Against conventional models, however, its mean AUC contrast was negative on the mixed panel (−0.0051 under original training; −0.0071 after removal) and positive on the survey-only panel (+0.0154 and +0.0187). TSS retained a further distinction: pseudoabsence removal changed the target-only contrast from +0.0477 to −0.0182 on the mixed panel, but from +0.0550 to +0.0097 on the survey-only panel (Table 2b).

## Discussion

The clearest bird result is that the neural model's small average advantage in the withheld regions was available without learning from other species. Fine-tuning exceeded tuned conventional models by +0.0028 AUC, while a network with the same architecture trained only on the target exceeded them by +0.0032. Learning across species improved development predictions for both birds and mainland mammals, but neither experiment retained a positive mean gain from pretraining in its new region. A comparison with conventional models alone would have missed this distinction and could have credited pretraining for an advantage already present in the target-only network.

The distinction matters because borrowing information across species is an attractive response to uneven biodiversity data. Multi-species embeddings, common-to-rare transfer and pretrained representations have already shown predictive value [@chen2017; @brun2024; @bourel2025; @ovaskainen2025; @lange2025]. The positive mainland and bird development gains are consistent with that promise. With the full auxiliary pool, the bird frozen-encoder control improved on target-only prediction on average within the training domain. Useful shared information was therefore available even without adapting the encoder to each target. The geographic assessments ask a further question: will the extra information help when the target model leaves the domain in which it was developed? Excluding both the focal species from pretraining and the assessment regions from all fitting, while retaining a matched target-only control, makes that added value visible. Here it varied among species and regions, including a positive mean in NCP, rather than yielding the consistent average benefit suggested by development performance.

Model averaging offered a more substantial improvement in withheld-region bird discrimination. Combining neural and conventional predictions improved the average whether or not the network had been pretrained. For a modeller seeking useful predictions, this suggests comparing complementary learners before assuming that a larger auxiliary dataset is the route to better performance. The neural model remained useful in an ensemble even where pretraining added little on average. This dependence on the task and on the fitted learners is consistent with broader SDM comparisons [@norberg2019; @valavi2022].

The learning curves also show why data efficiency needs an ecological interpretation. Fine-tuning gained more over conventional models when the primary bird panel was subsampled, but those targets remained well recorded even at the smallest fraction. In the least-recorded third, fine-tuning exceeded conventional models by only +0.0006 mean AUC. A model that uses a fraction of an abundant dataset efficiently has not necessarily solved the problem of predicting a poorly recorded species. Tests centred on those species are needed to establish the value of pretraining for rare-species applications.

The evaluation diagnostics help explain what a performance score can tell us. Bird AUC fell sharply between development and withheld regions, which could suggest a severe failure of geographic transfer. Yet re-scoring unchanged development predictions within regions produced a decline of similar magnitude. Restricting the comparison removed broad environmental contrasts between distant presences and background cells. Absolute AUC was therefore a poor guide to how much skill had been lost through transfer. The relative benefit of pretraining remained positive within the development regions, so this extent effect did not account for its disappearance from the mean withheld-region result. The two findings together favour comparisons among methods on the same observations. They do not quantify a causal geographic penalty, because the development and withheld-region assessments also differ in fitted models, environmental composition and recording support.

The negative observations changed the judgement more fundamentally: they could change which model ranked first. The bird advantage over conventional models declined as background recording richness increased. In Tasmania, removing pseudoabsences—3.3% of training rows, or 16.4% of training negatives—reduced performance against the original mixture but improved performance against survey-supported negatives alone. Restricting evaluation to those survey negatives also reversed the mean AUC ranking of fine-tuning and conventional models. Both evaluations reuse the same presence records, yet ask the models to distinguish them from different kinds of negative evidence. The choice has spatial consequences too: altering the training negatives changed which cells received the highest scores. Defining the negative class is thus part of specifying the prediction problem, with consequences for how a map should be interpreted and used.

What caused these sensitivities remains unresolved. Recording richness combines survey activity, community richness and environmental composition. Removing pseudoabsences changes class balance, spatial support and target fitting; reselected thresholds add a calibration change for TSS. Shared representations could encode environmental correlates of recording, or fine-tuning could amplify associations particular to the training domain. Feature distortion is possible in other machine-learning settings [@kumar2022], but these data cannot distinguish those mechanisms. The survey-only comparison is also post hoc and has sparse negative support for some species. It reveals sensitivity to the source of negative evidence, without establishing that either response construction represents ecological absence more accurately.

The scope of the transfer result is equally specific. We tested tabular environmental summaries, whereas spatial context and imagery can supply information that these inputs lack [@deneu2021]. The bird comparison also uses an architecture selected for fine-tuning, with no separate architecture search for the target-only network. Auxiliary-pool size changes the number of records and the species represented together. Finally, shared taxa, training pools and geography make the experiments and species contrasts dependent. The near-zero bird mean describes the observed panel; it is not evidence that the two training strategies are equivalent. These limits leave room for pretraining to help under other representations, species selections and prediction tasks.

The next tests should address those possibilities directly. Spatial habitat inputs would test whether richer environmental information improves geographic transfer. Observation-aware models and independent survey data could help separate habitat relationships from recording processes [@warton2013; @johnston2021]. These hypotheses now need fresh geographic assessments, with selection and evaluation rules fixed before outcomes are examined. For the present bird data, the small average neural advantage in withheld regions was largely available from the target-only network. Establishing that distinction changes the question from whether a pretrained model predicts well to how much learning from other species improves predictions where they are needed.

## Data and code availability

Analysis source, design records and aggregate evidence are maintained in the dSDM repository (<https://github.com/BWBrook/dSDM>). Recorded input, source and output hashes connect the reported summaries to the accepted analyses. Raw responses, row-level predictions, camera identifiers and precise locations are held outside the tracked aggregate evidence.
